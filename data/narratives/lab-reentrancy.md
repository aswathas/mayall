The reproduction confirms a classic re-entrancy drain against `ReentrancyVault`, and the trace leaves no room for a benign reading of the sequence.

The flaw is an ordering violation in `withdraw()`. The function reads the caller's recorded balance, transfers that ETH to the caller, and only afterwards writes `balances[msg.sender] = 0`. Because the state write happens after the external call, the caller's `receive()` hook runs while the vault still believes the balance is outstanding. The attacker's contract uses that window to re-enter `withdraw()` six times inside a single transaction. The trace shows `ReentrancyVault::withdraw()` invoked seven times — the initial call plus six re-entries — each one nesting deeper than the last, with `ReentrancyAttacker::receive()` sitting between every pair. That nesting pattern is the signature of the exploit; the detector raises it as Reentrancy at 97% confidence.

The money movement is unambiguous. The vault's balance fell from 10 ETH to 4 ETH across the transaction, a deficit of 6 ETH, and the attacker's contract balance rose by exactly the same 6 ETH over its 1 ETH seed deposit. Of that 6 ETH, 1 ETH is the attacker's own capital making a round trip; the net value extracted from the pool's other depositors is 5 ETH. The storage diff shows the accounting consequence: `totalWithdrawn` moves from 0 to 6 ETH while `totalDeposited` sits at 11 ETH, so the vault's books claim 11 ETH against a physical balance of 4 ETH. The contract is left insolvent by 7 ETH and the depositor who trusted it cannot be made whole.

What makes this more than a textbook example is that it needs no flash loan, no oracle, no privileged key and no multi-transaction sequencing. A single funded contract, one call, and the bug does the rest. The fix is the checks-effects-interactions ordering — decrement the balance before the transfer — or a reentrancy mutex on the withdrawal path. Until then, any balance in the vault is available to whoever can be paid twice.
